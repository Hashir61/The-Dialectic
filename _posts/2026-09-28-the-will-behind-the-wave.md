The relation of Schopenhauer to quantum mechanics is not one of anticipation in any crude sense. He died in 1860, forty years before Planck introduced the quantum of action, and nothing in *Die Welt als Wille und Vorstellung* predicts a wave function, a commutation relation, or a spectral line. The relation is of a different and philosophically more interesting kind. It holds between an account of what knowledge of nature can and cannot be, worked out by transcendental reflection alone, and a physical theory whose founders were driven by the internal pressure of their own results toward a substantially similar account. Some of them reached it by reading Schopenhauer directly; others by way of Kant, from whom Schopenhauer himself began. Where the influence is documented it will be stated as influence, and where the connection is only structural it will be stated as affinity; the argument is stronger for keeping the two apart.

## I. The Primacy of Will — A Philosophy Ahead of Its Time

Schopenhauer's system opens with a single sentence, and everything that follows is a consequence of taking it seriously:

> Die Welt ist meine Vorstellung.

The world as given — extended in space, successive in time, bound together by causal law — exists only as representation *for* a subject, and subject and object are the correlative halves of one structure, neither thinkable without the other.[^1] From Kant he accepts that space, time, and causality are forms contributed by the knowing mind, but he reduces Kant's twelve categories to causality alone and grounds the whole apparatus in the principle of sufficient reason, which his dissertation of 1813 had analysed as having a fourfold root. Every object of possible experience stands under this principle, and matter itself is, on this analysis, nothing over and above causality apprehended objectively: its being consists in its acting.

The decisive departure from Kant concerns the thing-in-itself. Kant had inferred it as the unknown cause of appearances, an inference Schopenhauer rejected as an application of causality beyond the domain in which it is valid. His alternative begins from the observation that the knowing subject is never merely a spectator. The subject's own body is given to it twice: as an object among objects, and from within, immediately, as the experience of willing. The Will so disclosed is the thing-in-itself, and the world of representation is its objectification. Because the Will lies outside space, time, and the principle of sufficient reason, it is groundless and undivided; plurality belongs to the phenomenal order alone, to what he calls, borrowing the Indian term, the *principium individuationis*, the veil of Maya.

Three consequences bear directly on what follows. The principle of sufficient reason governs representation and nothing else. Science, which operates wholly within representation, is therefore what Schopenhauer terms aetiology: it specifies the lawful succession of states while leaving untouched what a natural force *is*, and he reads gravitation, electricity, and chemical affinity as the lowest grades of the Will's objectification, primitives that physics may relate to one another but not derive. And materialism is diagnosed as the error of a philosophy that begins from the objectively given — matter — and forgets that it is given only to a subject, the one term that cannot appear in the picture it draws. This is also the fault line along which Schopenhauer's quarrel with the Hegelian identification of concept and being runs, a line examined through Schelling's critique in [On Hegel: A Critique of the Dialectical System]({{ '/philosophy/on-hegel/' | relative_url }}).

## II. Schrödinger's Debt: Where Wave Functions Meet Will

<div class="fig fig-right" markdown="0">
<img src="{{ '/assets/img/articles/schrodinger.jpg' | relative_url }}" alt="Portrait of Erwin Schrödinger">
<p class="cap">Erwin Schrödinger (1887–1961).</p>
</div>

Schrödinger's debt to Schopenhauer is the best documented in the history of the theory, and it is in one respect stranger than it first appears: the philosophy precedes the physics. He read Schopenhauer, and through his example the Upanishads, in the years around the First World War, and by 1925 he had committed to paper the metaphysical position later published as *Meine Weltansicht*, months before the wave equation was written down in early 1926.[^2] The author of the theory's most widely used equation came to it already convinced that the plurality of individual things and persons is appearance only.

The doctrine, in Schrödinger's formulation, is that consciousness is not a plurality. Each experiencing subject finds itself as one; the inference to a multitude of minds rests on the multiplicity of bodies, and bodies belong to the represented world. What Schopenhauer had called the *principium individuationis* Schrödinger accepts as the mechanism that generates the appearance of many out of one, and he adopts from the Vedanta the formula that Schopenhauer had made central to his ethics, *tat tvam asi*, "thou art that."[^3] To this he adds an epistemological argument of his own. Scientific objectivity, he observes in *Mind and Matter*, is purchased by what he calls the principle of objectivation: the subject of cognition is deliberately removed from the model of nature so that a shared, observer-independent description becomes possible. The cost is that the subject cannot then be found in the model. Sensation and thought are absent from the physical picture not because they are unreal but because the picture was constructed by excluding the one who draws it. It is Schopenhauer's critique of materialism restated in the vocabulary of twentieth-century physics: the subject is the condition of every object and is therefore never itself among them.

The equation itself, `iħ ∂ψ/∂t = Ĥψ`, is linear and deterministic. It governs the evolution of a state vector, and for a system of *N* particles that state is defined not in the three-dimensional space of ordinary perception but in a configuration space of 3*N* dimensions. The fundamental entity of the theory is thus not a representation in the Schopenhauerian sense, an object extended in space and time and given to a subject, but an abstract structure from which representable predictions are extracted. The definite outcome of a measurement is not deducible from ψ by the dynamics; only its probability is, through the rule Born supplied in 1926. That an outcome occurs, and that this one rather than another occurs, is not grounded in the state from which it arises. A philosopher formed by Schopenhauer will recognise the shape: a lawful, deterministic order of the representable, and at its boundary an event that the principle of sufficient reason cannot reach.

Schrödinger did not welcome this consequence, and the fact is essential to any honest account of his debt. He hoped the wave function would prove a real physical field, objected to the statistical reading and to quantum jumps, and in 1935 published the cat paradox as a reductio of the interpretation Bohr and Heisenberg were consolidating. In the same paper he introduced the term *Verschränkung*, entanglement, for the fact that two systems which have interacted cannot afterward be assigned independent states, and he judged this not one but the characteristic trait of the theory.[^4] The non-separability is now experimentally secure. Bell's theorem of 1964 showed that no local hidden-variable account can reproduce the correlations quantum mechanics predicts, and a line of experiments by Clauser, Aspect, and Zeilinger, recognised by the Nobel Prize of 2022, has confirmed the violation of Bell's inequalities. It would be an overreach to say that Schrödinger offered entanglement as proof of his metaphysics, and an evasion to ignore how closely the physical fact resembles the philosophical claim: that separateness in space belongs to how the world is represented and not, at bottom, to what is represented.

A final distinction prevents the identification from being made too easily. Schrödinger's singular is *consciousness*, the Vedantic Atman; Schopenhauer's singular is *Will*, blind and without knowledge, of which intellect is a late and instrumental product. The physicist retained the monism and transformed its substance. The divergence matters because it determines which interpretations of quantum mechanics may claim Schopenhauer's authority, a question taken up in the fifth section.

## III. Heisenberg, Uncertainty, and the Unknowable Thing-in-Itself

<div class="fig fig-left" markdown="0">
<img src="{{ '/assets/img/articles/heisenberg.jpg' | relative_url }}" alt="Portrait of Werner Heisenberg">
<p class="cap">Werner Heisenberg (1901–1976).</p>
</div>

Heisenberg's route to the same region was different in kind. No comparable documentary trail links him to Schopenhauer; his formation was scientific, mathematical, and classical, and the philosophical resources he drew upon were Kant, Plato, and Aristotle. The convergence is all the more informative for having been reached from a different starting point.

The starting point was a refusal. In the paper of 1925 that inaugurated matrix mechanics, Heisenberg proposed to eliminate from atomic theory those quantities that no observation could reach, above all the electron's orbit in space and time, and to construct the theory from relations among observables: the frequencies and intensities of spectral lines. The quantities so constructed do not commute; in the canonical case `x̂p̂ − p̂x̂ = iħ`. The uncertainty relation of 1927, `Δx·Δp ≥ ħ/2` in the form later made precise by Kennard, is the quantitative expression of that non-commutativity, and its interpretation was Heisenberg's central philosophical labour. His paper is titled, tellingly, *Über den anschaulichen Inhalt* — on the *intuitive* or perceptual content — of quantum-theoretical kinematics and mechanics. *Anschauung* is Kant's word for sensible intuition, and the paper's question is whether the space-time picture of the world, Kant's form of outer intuition, can be preserved at the atomic scale. His answer is that it can be preserved only partially, and at a price.

The price is stated with precision in the concluding section of the 1927 paper. The classical law of causality asserts that if the present is known exactly, the future can be computed. Heisenberg observes that what fails is not the conclusion but the premise: the present cannot be known exactly, since position and momentum are not simultaneously determinate to arbitrary precision. Causality is not refuted by a counterexample; its antecedent condition becomes unsatisfiable.[^5] The Kantian resonance is unmistakable: space, time, and causality are conditions under which objects can be described at all, and quantum theory reveals that they cannot be jointly satisfied by any single experimental arrangement.

In *Physics and Philosophy*, Heisenberg makes the Kantian reference explicit and revises it. The concepts of classical physics remain a priori in a functional sense, because the description of any experiment must be given in them; but the a priori is only relative, since quantum theory has shown their domain of validity to be bounded. He also replaces the classical notion of the state of an object with an Aristotelian one: the wave function expresses a *potentia*, a tendency toward events, whose passage into actuality occurs at the registration of a result. And he concludes, in a formulation that Schopenhauer would have recognised, that what we observe is not nature itself but "nature exposed to our method of questioning."

The limit of the parallel should be stated as exactly as the parallel itself. Heisenberg, like Kant, withholds. The reality behind the phenomena is for him no object of positive doctrine, and he identifies it with nothing. Schopenhauer's contribution to the Kantian inheritance is precisely the willingness to say what the thing-in-itself is, on the strength of the one point at which the subject is acquainted with something not in the form of an external object. Physics, as Heisenberg conceived it, has neither the means nor the mandate to follow. What it shares with Schopenhauer is the negative thesis: that the causal, spatiotemporal description is the form of our knowing and not the intrinsic structure of the known.

## IV. Planck's Quantum and the Collapse of Classical Certainty

<div class="fig fig-right fig-sm" markdown="0">
<img src="{{ '/assets/img/articles/planck.jpg' | relative_url }}" alt="Portrait of Max Planck">
<p class="cap">Max Planck (1858–1947).</p>
</div>

On 14 December 1900 Planck presented to the German Physical Society a derivation of the black-body radiation law that required the energy exchanged between matter and radiation at frequency ν to occur in integral multiples of a fixed unit, `ε = hν`.[^6] The derivation was undertaken to account for the measurements of Rubens and Kurlbaum, which showed that Wien's law failed at long wavelengths, while the classical equipartition argument, soon articulated by Rayleigh and Jeans, fails at the opposite end of the spectrum, predicting a divergent energy density at high frequency. Planck reached his result reluctantly. Three decades afterward he described the step as an act of desperation, a concession to the statistical methods of Boltzmann, which he had long resisted because they made the second law a statement of probability rather than of necessity. He intended the quantum as a property of the resonators in the wall of the cavity and expected that a more careful classical analysis would eventually absorb it.

That expectation was not fulfilled, and the failure is philosophically instructive. The quantum of action, *h*, whose value is now fixed by definition at exactly 6.62607015 × 10⁻³⁴ joule-seconds, enters physics as a datum. It can be measured with increasing precision, but no deeper theory derives it. It is, in Schopenhauer's terminology, the kind of thing an aetiology can use and cannot explain: an irreducible magnitude at which physical explanation terminates in a brute fact. Its consequence for the metaphysics of continuity was equally grave. Since Leibniz, physics had operated under the maxim that nature makes no leaps; the quantum of action introduced discontinuity into the elementary process, and with it the first breach in the picture of a world evolving smoothly and deterministically from its prior state.

Planck himself held to the classical ideal longer than most of the physicists who followed him. He remained a realist and a determinist by conviction, and his lecture of 1923 on the essence of free will argues, in effect, that the will is causally determined and yet subjectively free, a position with recognisable Schopenhauerian features. The philosopher's presence in this circle was more direct than that. In his address for Planck's sixtieth birthday in 1918, Einstein invoked Schopenhauer on the flight from everyday life as one of the strongest motives of scientific research, and in 1930 he named Schopenhauer's maxim that a man can do as he wills but cannot will as he wills a lifelong source of consolation and tolerance. Einstein drew from it a strict determinism of the phenomenal world, and it is among the ironies of the period that this determinism was turned against the very theory in which Schopenhauer's transcendental analysis found its closest physical counterpart.

## V. The Noumenal Substratum
The Copenhagen interpretation is less a single doctrine than a composite. The name was popularised by Heisenberg in the 1950s, and historians such as Don Howard have shown that it fuses positions of Bohr and Heisenberg that were never wholly identical.[^7] Its core elements are nevertheless stable. The quantum state encodes probabilities for measurement outcomes, according to the Born rule. Complementarity, which Bohr presented at Como in 1927, holds that mutually exclusive experimental arrangements disclose aspects of a quantum object, such as wave-like and particle-like behaviour, that cannot be combined into a single classical picture yet must both be invoked for a complete account. The measuring apparatus must be described in classical terms, since only these serve to communicate what was done and observed. And the passage from a superposition `|ψ⟩ = Σ cᵢ|aᵢ⟩` to a single result `|aₖ⟩`, with probability `|cₖ|²`, is not a consequence of the unitary dynamics but an additional postulate, which is the measurement problem in its standard form.

Read as epistemology, the doctrine reproduces the architecture of the first section point by point. The object exists in the theory only relative to a means of knowing it: Bohr's insistence that the quantum of action makes a sharp separation between object and instrument unattainable is the physical counterpart of the claim that subject and object are correlative. The forms of description are conditions of knowledge rather than intrinsic features of the known: Bohr held classical concepts to be indispensable, and their complementary application to exhaust what can be said. And the reality beneath the phenomena is declared inaccessible to these forms: physics, in the remark Aage Petersen attributed to Bohr, concerns what we can say about nature and not how nature is. The theorem of Kochen and Specker sharpens the point, for it shows that a system cannot be assigned definite values for all its observables independently of the measurement context. Properties are then defined relative to an experimental arrangement and not possessed absolutely, the physical analogue of Schopenhauer's denial that an object subsists apart from a subject.

The alignment is not, however, complete, and a defence of Schopenhauer's relevance is weakened by claiming that it is. His own position holds the principle of sufficient reason to be exceptionless within the phenomenal world: in his essay on the freedom of the will, action follows from character and motive with the necessity of any physical event. Quantum randomness, taken at face value, contradicts this. What it vindicates is the more fundamental transcendental thesis, that causal explanation is a form of representation and not a property of the thing-in-itself; the breakdown of that form at the boundary of the phenomenal is what the doctrine leads one to expect. The vindication is of the status of causality, not of its universality. A second limit concerns consciousness. The von Neumann–Wigner reading, in which the collapse is completed by a conscious observer, appears at first sight the most Schopenhauerian, but Bohr's account made no appeal to it, and Schopenhauer's own ordering would reject it also: for him intellect is secondary to Will and its instrument, so that an interpretation which grants consciousness priority over the physical world inverts his hierarchy.

What remains is the sober conclusion that the Copenhagen interpretation is Schopenhauerian in its epistemology and neutral in its metaphysics. It affirms that what we know of nature is nature as represented, under forms that are ours, and it declines, as Kant did and Schopenhauer did not, to say what lies beneath. The philosophy offers a candidate, the Will; the physics supplies no means of testing it.

## VI. Conclusion: Philosophy Did Not Follow Science — It Preceded It

On the evidence reviewed, philosophy did not follow science here; it preceded it, and the claim can now be stated with the qualifications it requires. The historical influence is documented in two cases: Schrödinger, whose monism and whose critique of objectivation derive substantially from Schopenhauer and the tradition he opened to European thought, and Einstein, whose determinism drew on Schopenhauer's maxim on the will. The structural affinity is established in three others: Heisenberg's relativised Kantian a priori, Planck's irreducible quantum of action as the residue of an aetiology, and the Copenhagen construction of the physical object as relative to the means of its observation. None of these establishes that the thing-in-itself is Will. Nothing in the formalism could, and the physicists who came closest to the thesis, Schrödinger included, took the metaphysics from outside physics and not from within it.

What the record does establish is narrower and more durable. In 1818–19 a philosophy was published which held that the world of space, time, and causal law is a world of representation; that the representable order is lawful while the ground of its existence is not subject to that law; that plurality belongs to the form of appearance rather than to what appears; and that a science confined to representation cannot, by its own procedures, account for the subject who represents. A century later, physicists arriving from experiment and mathematics, with no commitment to this philosophy and in some cases with active resistance to it, were compelled to formulate a theory in which each of these claims has a precise physical counterpart. That the counterpart was reached independently is what gives the correspondence its weight. A framework that identifies the structure of a later discovery without having anticipated any of its content has earned a serious hearing, and it has earned it in the very domain, the limits of what can be known of nature, in which it always claimed its authority.
